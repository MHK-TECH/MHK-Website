Problem 01: Riemann Hypothesis

All non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function lie on the critical line with real part 1/2. Proving this would give us the deepest understanding of prime number distribution we've ever had.

Why it matters

The zeta function encodes information about prime numbers. Every result about zeta is a result about primes. Primes are the foundation of modern cryptography.

My approach

Training neural networks to detect patterns in zeta zero distributions. The first 10 trillion zeros have been computed. They all lie on the critical line. But computation isn't proof.

The interesting finding: zeta zeros follow the same statistics as eigenvalues of random matrices. This connection between number theory and quantum physics is one of the deepest mysteries in mathematics.

Problem 02: P vs NP

Can every problem whose solution is verified quickly also be solved quickly? This is the central question of theoretical computer science. If P = NP, most modern cryptography breaks. If P ≠ NP, we need to prove it.

Why it matters

It determines what's computationally feasible. It affects cryptography, optimization, drug discovery, logistics — everything that involves finding solutions.

My approach

Studying SAT solvers and their limits. Where do they struggle? What structure makes a problem hard? Building benchmark datasets to push computational boundaries.

Modern SAT solvers can handle millions of variables. They shouldn't be able to, if P ≠ NP. Understanding why they work so well might give us insights into the P vs NP question.

Problem 03: Navier-Stokes

Do smooth solutions always exist for the Navier-Stokes equations in three dimensions? These equations describe fluid flow — water, air, blood, plasma. Every weather prediction, every aircraft design, every blood flow simulation depends on them.

Why it matters

Turbulence is physics' deepest unsolved mystery. We can simulate it approximately, but we don't understand it fundamentally. A proof would revolutionize computational fluid dynamics.

My approach

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs). The Navier-Stokes equations are part of the loss function. The model can't learn unphysical solutions.

# Physics-Informed Neural Network
class PINN(nn.Module):
    def physics_loss(self, x, y, t):
        u, v, p = self.forward(x, y, t)
        # Automatic differentiation for derivatives
        u_t = torch.autograd.grad(u, t, ...)[0]
        u_x = torch.autograd.grad(u, x, ...)[0]
        # Navier-Stokes residual
        residual = u_t + u*u_x + v*u_y + p_x - visc*(u_xx + u_yy)
        return (residual ** 2).mean()

Problem 04: Birch & Swinnerton-Dyer

Connect elliptic curves to L-functions. Specifically, predict the rank of an elliptic curve from its L-function behavior at s=1. This conjecture bridges algebraic geometry and number theory.

Why it matters

Elliptic curves are fundamental to number theory and cryptography (ECC). Understanding their structure has implications for factoring, encryption, and algebraic geometry.

My approach

Large-scale computation. Computing L-functions for thousands of elliptic curves and looking for patterns that might suggest a proof strategy.

Problem 05: Hodge Conjecture

Every Hodge class on a projective variety is a rational linear combination of algebraic cycle classes. This is topology meeting algebraic geometry — understanding the shape of mathematical objects through equations.

Why it matters

This connects two fundamental areas of mathematics. A proof would give us deep insights into the relationship between geometry and algebra.

My approach

Topological data analysis (TDA). Persistent homology gives us tools to study the shape of data. I'm applying these tools to algebraic varieties.

What this teaches me

Working on these problems develops skills that transfer directly to data science and engineering:

  • Pattern recognition. You start seeing structure everywhere. This is the core skill of data science.
  • Computational thinking. You learn to think about what's computable and what's not. This matters for model design.
  • Mathematical rigor. You learn to be precise. Vague thinking doesn't work in mathematics.
  • Intellectual humility. These problems have defeated the best minds for 25+ years. You learn to respect difficulty.
  • Cross-domain thinking. The connections between these problems are deep. Thinking across domains leads to insights.
